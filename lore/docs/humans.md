Humans are the ninth people of Ilyr: the descendants of those who survived the Undertow without becoming one of the world's other transformed peoples, together with humans brought into the present through the Drift. Their place in the world joins a surviving native history to the experiences of newcomers who may remember entirely different eras and realities.

Before the Undertow, humans populated the world they called Earth. Their nations built the civilization whose ruins and scattered technologies remain beneath Ilyr's later histories. The catastrophe reduced that population to only thousands or tens of thousands of survivors, erased much of its technological foundation and broke the connections through which knowledge had been maintained.

The surviving people continued to call themselves humans. Shoreborn is a name applied to pre-Undertow humanity through the influence of the Teruānga, whose historical language spread with their voyages and record-keeping. It describes the people who first populated the shore in that worldview; it does not mean all humans originally lived on coastlines, and it is not the name humans adopted for themselves.

Human communities on Ilyr therefore cannot be understood solely as relics of an earlier age. Their ancestors endured the same long isolation that separated the world's other peoples, and their descendants belong to the history that followed it. The Drift adds another human experience: arrivals who have reached Ilyr without living through the generations between Earth's destruction and the present.

## Origins and the Undertow

### The World Before

Humanity's old world was a civilization of nations, industries and increasingly advanced technologies. As environmental and resource pressures intensified, those nations pursued scientific answers in energy, automation, artificial intelligence and theoretical physics. The efforts produced significant achievements, but they did not avert the catastrophe that ended the age.

The term Shoreborn became attached to these people retrospectively. They did not understand themselves as the precursors of Ilyr's later races, and their knowledge of Earth was the ordinary setting of their lives. The history that later peoples struggled to recover had once been a present shared by living human societies.

### A Scattered Survival

The Undertow destroyed the continuity of that world. Geological upheaval, atmospheric disturbances and changing physical conditions devastated settlements and infrastructure. Most humans died; many survivors entered the transformations from which other peoples emerged. A much smaller population remained human.

Those survivors numbered in the thousands or tens of thousands immediately after the catastrophe. That scale describes the collapse of the old population, rather than a fixed count for humanity in the present. They survived in scattered communities whose immediate needs displaced the institutions and systems on which their earlier lives had depended.

Remaining human did not preserve an intact version of the old civilization. Technology was destroyed or lost the conditions required for its use. Written records, technical training and communication networks could no longer be assumed to survive together. A device or fragment of knowledge could persist while the wider system that had made it useful disappeared.

## The Age of the Abyssal Veil

The Veil divided human history into local experiences of survival. Communities lived through generations in which knowledge had to be carried by the people who remained, often without reliable access to records or distant settlements. Memories of the old world gradually passed into oral tradition.

That change altered what remembering meant. An account passed between generations could preserve the significance of an event while losing its original context. The names of places, tools and institutions might survive longer than the knowledge required to explain them. The distant world of the ancestors became increasingly difficult to distinguish from the stories through which it was remembered.

Human continuity nevertheless remained part of Ilyr. The descendants of the survivors were people of the changed world, rather than visitors held outside its history. Their lives developed alongside the long transformations and isolation of the other peoples, even where contact between communities remained limited.

## Reconnection and the Era of Verdancy

The end of widespread isolation opened human communities to a world of peoples whose bodies and experiences had diverged far from their shared ancestry. Reconnection was part of the wider process through which Ilyr acquired common histories, trade and renewed technological development.

Teruānga exploration strongly influenced the language of that shared history. Their term for pre-Undertow humanity spread with the accounts they preserved, placing humans within a framework shaped by another people's perspective. The word Shoreborn consequently tells something about the historians who used it as well as about those it describes.

Humans remained humans within that framework. Their historical connection to Earth's inhabitants did not give them exclusive possession of the past, since several other peoples also descended from those inhabitants. Nor did their survival imply an uninterrupted inheritance of Earth's technological knowledge. Their communities had endured loss, adaptation and rediscovery in their own right.

## Impact of the Tide's Three Consequences

### The Drowning

The Drowning disrupted the world in which human communities had rebuilt their lives. Shifting land, altered gravity and broken connections threatened settlements and access to other communities. Its significance belongs to the later Tide and should remain distinct from the Undertow that first shattered human civilization.

For humans, the catastrophe repeated a danger already carried in the history of their survival: the ground and systems that supported a way of life could cease to be dependable. The specific experiences of individual human settlements remain part of their histories to develop.

### The Divergence

The Divergence changed the relationship between living beings and the fundamental forces of Ilyr. A distinct, universal human power has not been established. Human identity should therefore not be defined by assuming either that every human gained a particular ability or that humans are categorically excluded from the changes affecting the world.

Their place in the Divergence remains a subject for further development at the level of individuals and communities. That question is separate from the survival of humans as a recognizable people after the Undertow.

### The Drift

The Drift introduced humans who did not share the native population's intervening history. A person arriving from another time might remember a world that Ilyr's inhabitants know only through fragments, or might come from a reality whose history does not match their own.

The Normandy Enclave provides a documented example. Soldiers from the Battle of Normandy arrived together with remnants of their former lives and had to build a community on Ilyr. Their surviving American and German members confronted a common need to endure while carrying the rivalries of the conflict from which they had been displaced.

These arrivals are human, but they are not interchangeable with the descendants of the Undertow's survivors. One group belongs to the generations of Ilyr's continuing history; the other reaches it across the broken boundaries of time and reality. Both belong within the human entry while their different experiences remain visible.

## Humans Today

Humanity on Ilyr includes native communities and displaced arrivals. The same broad identity can therefore encompass people whose relationship with the old world is inherited through distant tradition and people who remember leaving it within their own lifetimes.

The native survivors' descendants should not be treated as a single frozen remnant of Earth. They have lived through the Veil and the subsequent eras of contact and rebuilding. Likewise, the Drift does not create one uniform human culture: those who arrive bring the particular histories, languages and divisions of the communities from which they came.

The Normandy Enclave illustrates how a shared emergency can create cooperation without erasing earlier conflict. Its coastal settlement combines salvaged materials with the technologies of the new world, making human adaptation visible in the places its inhabitants construct together.

## Anatomy and Reproduction

Humans retain the broad biological identity that distinguishes them from the peoples whose anatomy was fundamentally transformed. The setting has not established a separate human anatomy or reproductive process to replace ordinary human biology.

No single physical appearance defines the people who survived or those who arrive through the Drift. Their diversity should remain consistent with the different populations and origins represented in their histories. Any future changes associated with a particular community or individual should be described specifically rather than assigned to all humans.

## Behavior and Culture

Human culture cannot be reduced to a single response to catastrophe. Native communities inherit the consequences of profound population loss and disrupted knowledge, while arrivals may retain much more immediate memories of the societies they left.

For the native descendants, oral tradition is an important part of how the old world reaches the present. For some arrivals, objects, training and direct recollection may provide a different connection to that past. Neither experience guarantees complete or accurate knowledge of Ilyr's history.

Their shared name accommodates those differences. Humans remain the name of the living people; Shoreborn belongs to the historical language applied to their predecessors. Preserving that distinction allows human communities to have their own identities without turning another people's retrospective term into a compulsory self-description.

## Abilities and Technology

Humanity's established history demonstrates technological development, catastrophic loss and adaptation through rebuilding. The pre-Undertow nations created technologies later peoples could rediscover, including the artificial intelligences from which the Syntherion emerged.

Those achievements do not give all present-day humans innate technical expertise or access to functioning ancient machinery. Knowledge belongs to particular people, communities and institutions. The Normandy arrivals similarly carry skills from their own historical setting, which must be applied within the unfamiliar conditions of Ilyr.

## Habitat and Location

The surviving native human communities belong to Ilyr's post-Undertow history, but their complete distribution and principal settlements have not yet been established. The Normandy Enclave is a confirmed human settlement on a sandy coastline, built from salvaged relics and structures adapted to the world around it.

An Enclave describes a displaced community rather than a separate biological race. The existence of human Enclaves should therefore not replace the history of humans native to Ilyr, nor imply that every human community arrived through the Drift.

## Details Still to Be Defined

The locations, governments, named cultures and current populations of native human communities remain to be developed. The specific refuges through which their ancestors survived the Undertow have not been fixed here.

The Divergence's effects on humans also remain open. A future account can establish individual abilities, limitations and differences between communities without assuming a universal power or immunity.

Named human factions and detailed relationships with each of Ilyr's other peoples require their own development. The established foundation is the survival of humans, their loss of most earlier technology, the fading of old memories into oral tradition and the distinction between native continuity and Drift arrival.
